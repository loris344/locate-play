Geography guessing games are older than any one product — pin-the-location-on-a-map is a classic pub quiz and party game format that predates any digital version by decades. What's changed is the source material: panoramic street imagery, single photos, video clips, and now footage nobody built a game around before.

## Why geography guessing games attracts location-game players

The appeal is consistent across every format: a satisfying, immediate feedback loop (guess, reveal, see exactly how far off you were) combined with a skill that keeps improving the more you play. That loop is why the genre has stayed popular well beyond any single game — it's genuinely replayable in a way that most quiz formats aren't, because no two rounds look the same.

## Photo, video, Street View and scene-based formats

- **Street View / panoramic**: you can look around, sometimes move a short distance. Road signage, driving side, vegetation and infrastructure carry most of the signal.
- **Static photo**: one frame, no exploration. Usually outdoor and landscape-oriented, which keeps a decent amount of visual information even without movement.
- **Video clip**: a few seconds of footage, sometimes indoors. Motion and audio become usable clues that a still photo doesn't have.
- **Scene-based (this site's format)**: video clips that are frequently interior, which strips away most of the classic outdoor clues (roads, plants, sky) and replaces them with fixtures, furniture, and anything readable in the background.

Each format rewards a different balance of skills — panorama formats reward broad geographic knowledge, while single-frame and scene-based formats reward close attention to small, easy-to-miss detail.

## How map scoring and clue reading change by format

Distance-based scoring — points scale down the further your pin lands from the real location — is close to universal across the genre, which is part of why it feels familiar switching between formats. What changes is how much you can trust any single clue. A panorama gives you multiple corroborating details (a road sign AND a driving side AND vegetation); a single frame or clip might give you exactly one usable clue, which means the clue-reading has to be more careful and less confident by default.
