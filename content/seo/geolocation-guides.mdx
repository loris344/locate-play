Visual geolocation — working out where an image or video was captured using only what's visible in it — is a real discipline, not a game invention. Open-source investigators, journalists doing verification work, and geography hobbyists all use versions of the same technique; games like this one just turn it into a scored, replayable challenge.

## Why visual geolocation guide matters in visual geolocation

The reason this skill generalizes so well across completely different sources — travel photos, news footage, game screenshots, video clips — is that it isn't really about any one image type. It's a fixed set of clue categories (vegetation and climate, infrastructure and signage, architecture, language, light and shadow) applied to whatever's in frame. Once you've trained yourself to check those categories in order, the skill transfers to sources you've never guessed from before.

## How to read the clue correctly

A practical checklist, roughly in the order that tends to pay off fastest:

1. **Script and language** — even a single word narrows a huge amount of the world instantly, more than almost any other clue type.
2. **Climate and vegetation** — palm trees, snow, and specific crop types constrain latitude and, often, hemisphere.
3. **Infrastructure conventions** — road markings, utility poles, and electrical fixtures vary by country far more consistently than most people assume.
4. **Light direction and shadow length** — a rough tool for hemisphere and time-of-day, useful mainly as a tie-breaker rather than a primary clue.
5. **Architecture and building materials** — the softest signal on this list, because building styles travel and get copied across regions constantly.

## Common false positives and regional overlap

The most common mistake isn't misreading a clue — it's over-trusting one that happens to be ambiguous. Left-hand traffic doesn't uniquely identify a country; neither does a particular plant species that's been cultivated well outside its native range for a century. The fix is structural, not a matter of getting smarter at any one clue: require at least two independent categories to agree before you commit to more than a rough region.
