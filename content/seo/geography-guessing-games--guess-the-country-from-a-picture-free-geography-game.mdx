A single photo is a meaningfully different puzzle from a Street View round, even though both ask the same underlying question. Street View gives you a full panorama and sometimes a short walk; a photo gives you exactly one frame and nothing else — whatever's outside it doesn't exist for the purposes of the guess.

## Why guess the country from a picture attracts location-game players

The single-photo format strips the genre down to its purest version: no exploring, no second angle, just one image and everything you can extract from it. That constraint is exactly what makes it appealing to players who've gotten comfortable with panorama-based guessing and want a harder, more concentrated version of the same skill.

## Photo, video, Street View and scene-based formats

- **Single photo**: one frame, typically outdoor, usually landscape-oriented — road markings, vegetation, and architecture are your main tools.
- **Street View panorama**: full 360° context, sometimes limited movement — the most forgiving format because you can cross-reference multiple details in the same round.
- **Video clip**: motion and audio become usable on top of whatever's visible, but you lose the ability to "look around" a fixed frame.
- **Scene-based**: often interior, which removes most classic outdoor photo clues (vegetation, sky, road signage) entirely and replaces them with fixtures, furniture and anything readable in the background.

## How map scoring and clue reading change by format

Distance-based scoring is consistent across all of these, but your confidence going into a guess should scale with how much the format actually gives you. A single photo with a visible road sign or clearly identifiable vegetation zone can support a confident, narrow guess; a photo with none of that should get a broad, hedged guess instead — the scoring curve rewards a well-placed regional guess more than it punishes not knowing the exact city.
