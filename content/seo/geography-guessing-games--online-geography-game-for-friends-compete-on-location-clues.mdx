A geography game played alone and the same game played with friends reward slightly different things. Solo play rewards raw accuracy over many rounds; group play rewards being able to reason out loud and defend a guess in front of people who'll immediately tell you if they disagree.

## Why online geography game for friends attracts location-game players

The appeal of a group session is the disagreement itself — five people looking at the same clip and landing on five different guesses is more entertaining than the reveal alone. That only works cleanly with a format where everyone sees exactly the same fixed information, which is why single-frame and clip-based formats often work better for groups than panorama-based ones (where the person controlling the screen has an unfair information advantage from being the one panning around).

## Photo, video, Street View and scene-based formats

For group play specifically, a fixed photo or short clip is the more fair format — nobody controls the view, so nobody has an advantage from exploring more than anyone else got to see. A Street View panorama, by contrast, gives whoever's holding the controls a real edge, since they decide what gets looked at and for how long.

## How map scoring and clue reading change by format

Group rounds work best with a simple, visible scoring system everyone can track without effort — distance-based scoring displayed clearly after each round gives the group an obvious number to argue about, which is most of the fun. Complicated multi-factor scoring systems tend to kill the momentum of a group session; simple and immediate wins.
